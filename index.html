A letter from us

We are going home —
come build with us.

We are Ritu and Shree Kant, from Phalodi. We are building our home in Malar — our village in the Thar desert — in stone, lime and earth, by hand, the way this region has always built.

And we are looking for people to build it with us.

Not contractors. Not a crew. People who want to learn — to mix cob with their hands, to feel how lime plaster goes onto a wall, to understand why a stone sits where it sits. Skills that are thousands of years old and almost forgotten. Skills you will carry with you long after the build is done.

There is something that happens when you build with natural materials. The work is slow and physical and honest. At the end of a day you can see exactly what you made. That feeling — of having shaped something that will stand for a hundred years — is rare. We want to share it.

We are working with Tiny Farm Lab, architects who work in these techniques every day. They will be on site, teaching, guiding, making sure what gets built is built right. You will learn from people who truly know this craft.

Come for a few days, come for longer. No experience needed — only curiosity and willingness. Leave something of yourself in these walls. Come be part of this.

With love and mud on our hands,

The place

Malar, in the heart
of the Thar

Malar is a small village near Phalodi, in the Thar desert of Rajasthan. It is the kind of place where people still know how to build with the land — where quarried stone is stacked in lime mortar the same way it was three centuries ago.

The desert demands architecture that listens to it. Thick walls that absorb the day's heat and release it through cool nights. Small, shaded openings. A courtyard that catches the evening breeze. This is not a design choice — it is just how you build here if you are paying attention.

We are paying attention.

The House

Small, considered,

Around 1,100 square feet — a master bedroom, a child's room, a flexible guest room, a kitchen, a living space that opens to the verandah, and at the centre of it all, a courtyard open to the sky.

Every space has a reason to be where it is. The bedroom faces the coolest wind. The kitchen is close to the garden. The verandah is where inside and outside dissolve into each other.

No false ceiling to hide the timber. No tiles over the earth. Nothing between you and the material of the house.

What we're building with

Materials that come from here
and go back to here

Local Stone

Quarried nearby, set in lime mortar. The walls that have stood in Rajasthan for centuries look like this.

Lime Plaster

Multi-coat, hand-applied. Breathable and beautiful — it gets better as it ages, not worse.

Cob

Clay, sand and straw mixed by hand. The interior walls you press yourself will be part of our home forever.

Timber

Local or sustainably sourced. For the roof structure, door frames, and joinery.

Earth Floors

Compacted earth and lime. The oldest flooring in the world. Cool underfoot. Gets more beautiful over time.

Why it matters

Building this way is not
a trend. It's a necessity.

We chose stone, lime and earth because they are right for this climate and this place. But the more we learned, the more we realised this choice is also right for the planet — and that the two things are not separate.

Cement is one of the world's biggest climate problems

The production of cement and concrete accounts for nearly 8% of global CO₂ emissions — more than aviation. Every bag of cement poured into a wall carries that cost. Earth, stone and lime have no such burden. Lime actually absorbs CO₂ back as it cures over decades.

Concrete buildings in the desert don't work

A concrete box in Rajasthan traps heat — you need air conditioning running all day to make it liveable. A thick earthen wall does the opposite: it absorbs heat slowly through the day and releases it at night, keeping the inside cool without a single unit of electricity. Our grandparents knew this. We forgot.

Local materials mean local livelihoods

Stone is quarried nearby. Earth comes from the site itself. Lime is made in the region. Nothing is shipped from a factory far away. The money spent on materials stays in the local economy, and the skills required — stone masonry, lime plastering, cob — are ones that people here already carry.

These buildings last centuries — and heal themselves

Lime plaster is self-healing: hairline cracks close on their own as the material continues to cure. Earthen walls that crack can be repaired with water and mud. A concrete building that fails needs machinery. A lime and earth building that needs repair needs only hands and locally available material.

They are healthy to live in

Earthen and lime walls breathe — they regulate humidity naturally, preventing the damp and mould that sealed concrete walls trap. No synthetic resins, no chemical sealants, no off-gassing paints. The air inside an earthen home feels different. Calmer. Cleaner. The kind of home you actually want to grow old in.

The Thar has been doing this for 500 years

This is not an experiment. The stone havelis of Rajasthan, the earthen homes of the Thar, the lime-washed forts that still stand — they are proof that these materials work here, in this heat, in this wind, on this soil. We are not reinventing anything. We are remembering.

We are building one house. But if more families build this way — using what is local, what is living, what is honest — it adds up. This is what we can do. And it feels like exactly the right time to do it.

— Ritu & Shree Kant

How we'll build

Slowly, together,
the right way

The construction will happen in phases over the coming months. Tiny Farm Lab's architects will be on site, training the craftsmen — and us, and you — in each technique as it comes.

Setting out & foundation

Marking the land. Understanding where the sun falls, where the wind comes from, where the courtyard will catch the evening light.

Stone walls

The main walls go up in locally quarried stone and lime mortar. Heavy, cool, and unmistakably of this place. This is the most physical part of the build.

Roof & timber

The timber roof structure is placed. The building begins to feel like a building.

Cob interiors

The interior walls get their cob finish — the part we're most excited to share with visitors. You mix it with your hands and press it onto the wall. It stays there.

Lime plaster & floors

The final layer — lime plaster on the outside walls, earth floors inside. The house begins to look the way we always imagined it.

Garden, courtyard & systems

Planting, landscaping, solar, bio-digester, greywater. Making the house self-sufficient and settled into its land.
